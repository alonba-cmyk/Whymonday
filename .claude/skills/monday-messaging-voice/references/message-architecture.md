# Message Architecture

How Alon structures a message before a word of copy is written. This is the load-bearing layer. Get it right and the copy almost writes itself.

## 1. One controlling idea per asset

Every asset hangs on a single tension that every element proves and re-proves. Decide it first.

- Company level: **ambition vs. execution** — "Execution can finally catch up to ambition."
- Deck level: **people and agents, together** — stated in the hero and re-stated in the CTA.

**Test:** if a section doesn't advance the one idea, cut it or fold it. Open and close on the same idea so the asset feels sealed.

## 2. WHAT / WHY / HOW — architect the skeleton before prose

For strategy and positioning, lay the message out as a labeled matrix before writing sentences. From the GTM Vision exec summary:

| Category | Key message |
|---|---|
| WHAT — the new promise | Execution can finally catch up to ambition, on one platform where people and agents deliver business results. |
| WHY — the shift | AI makes it possible to move from selling a product to delivering actual business outcomes. |
| HOW — the offering | One platform, any business outcome. Agents execute, surfaces shape to any job, apps built to match how you work. |
| MARKET | Where we lead and where we're catching up. |
| WIN | The few things that set us apart. |

Write the grid, then dress each row in prose. Never start from a blank paragraph.

## 3. Sequence: Vision → Problem → Diagnosis → Solution → Proof → CTA

The fixed arc. The problem is **never first**. Lead with the aspiration so the reader is bought in before you name the pain.

1. **Vision** — the aspirational state ("How people and agents work together...").
2. **Problem** — framed as capacity, not capability ("Your team is already running at 100%").
3. **Diagnosis** — why the naive fix fails (the 4-part skeleton, below).
4. **Solution** — the reframe + the platform ("teammates, not tools" → Workspace, Context, Control).
5. **Proof** — social proof, analysts, Customer Zero.
6. **CTA** — closes on the controlling idea.

## 4. The 4-part problem skeleton (re-pointed per angle)

Diagnosis always comes in four parallel beats, and the beats are swapped to fit the specific argument while the skeleton stays.

- Isolation angle: **No shared workspace / No one managing them / No context / Siloed output.**
- Adoption angle: **Individual AI usage / Lack of adoption / No context / Siloed output.**

Keep the four-beat rhythm; re-point the beats to whatever this asset argues.

## 5. Symmetry: problem ↔ solution share a skeleton

Make the solution feel inevitable by mirroring the problem's structure. The 4-part diagnosis resolves into the pillars **Workspace · Context · Control** (where agents work, what they know, who governs them). Problem and answer rhyme.

## 6. Spine + front — the signature method

**This is the most "Alon" thing in the whole system.** He does not rewrite a message per audience. He keeps a stable core (the spine) and swaps only the entry point (the front) to match each audience's deepest desire.

Evidence across registers:
- Two pitch decks, back half identical word-for-word (Workspace·Context·Control → testimonials → 5,117 agents → The Result), different openings: one enters through the pain ("The Reality: your team is running at 100%"), one through the mission ("Our Mission... what's changed").
- One persona-site skeleton reused for Marketing and HR; only the copy is re-tuned.
- One email body, A/B-tested with two different hooks.
- Elevate session titles: same offering, hook bent to each department's appetite.

**How to apply:**
1. Write the spine once (the durable core argument + proof).
2. Identify the audience's deepest desire (IT wants to lead responsibly; PMO wants delivery; Enterprise wants transformation at scale; Marketing wants volume without a ceiling).
3. Swap only the front — the hook, the opening frame, the first example — to speak to that desire. Leave the spine intact.

## 7. Progression scaffolds — make an abstract shift feel inevitable

To sell a shift, Alon builds a scaffold of time or stages so it reads as a natural next step, not a leap:
- **Timeline:** AI waves 2022 → 2026.
- **Stages:** Evolution Stage 1 → 4.
- **A Day in the Life:** hour-by-hour (9:00 brief → 11:00 proposals ready → ...).

Use one when you need the reader to feel "this was always heading here."

## 8. Reframe by changing the question

The sharpest repositioning move: don't argue the old answer, change the question being asked.

> The question changed, from "which product do you need?" to "what do you want to achieve?"

## 9. How proof is handled

Proof is never a bare claim. It always carries one of these:

- **Specificity over superlative.** "5,117 agents built in 1 week," not "thousands."
- **Human root under the technical claim.** "each one started as someone's daily frustration."
- **Contrastive statistic (X, yet Y).** "90% of employees use personal AI tools, yet 95% of enterprise pilots show no measurable impact."
- **Claim paired with its source.** Every challenge→opportunity line cites an analyst (HBR, Gartner, Microsoft WTI).
- **Characters, not features.** Agents get proper names ("The Silence Disruptor," "Jedi PMM") so proof reads as teammates, not tooling.
- **Parallel contrastive proof headline.** "Recognized by analysts, loved by users." / "Don't take our word."

## 10. The "it was never about X" closer

A signature ending move: deny the obvious thesis, then assert the deeper one. Negation is allowed here (never as an opener).

> It was never about the AI. The differentiator won't be who has the best AI. It will be who owns the surface where AI meets real work.
